\begin{abstract}
\noindent
Sparse autoencoders (SAEs) are meant to decompose language-model activations into monosemantic,
human-interpretable features, but \emph{feature absorption}---in which a seemingly general feature
silently fails on a subset of tokens while a token-specific latent quietly carries the concept---
undermines that interpretation. Absorption is quantified in the literature almost exclusively on a
single family of tasks, ``does this token start with the letter $X$,'' and with two different metrics:
an older \emph{ablation} (behavioral) metric that attributes the model's answer to individual latents,
and the current SAEBench-standard \emph{projection} (representational) metric that decomposes a
concept direction into per-latent contributions. We ask a question that has, to our knowledge, not
been tested: do the two metrics still agree once absorption is measured on a structurally different,
non-spelling property? Running both metrics on the binary property \iscap{} across Gemma-2-2b and
Gemma-2-9b (Gemma Scope $16$k SAEs), we find that they \emph{disagree}. The projection metric reports
single-dominant-latent absorption whose point estimate is $3.6\times$ its random-direction null
($0.12$ vs.\ $0.03$ at the strict dominance ratio $R{=}3$, $n{=}130$; Wilson $95\%$ CI $[0.077,0.191]$,
which excludes the null point estimate but is marginal at this sample size), whereas the ablation
metric reports exactly zero absorption on \emph{every} candidate token on both models ($0/727$ on
Gemma-2-2b, $0/273$ on Gemma-2-9b). Two controls rule out the mundane
explanations: the zero is not caused by the model failing the task (Gemma-2-9b performs \iscap{} at
$0.91$ accuracy, versus $0.59$ for 2b, yet ablation absorption stays $0.0$) and not by a dead layer
(at the same 9b layer the ablation metric fires at $0.045$ for spelling, and at \emph{matched} task
accuracy ${\sim}0.9$ it fires at ${\sim}0.017$--$0.035$ for spelling while \iscap{} remains $0.0$). We
conclude that the field-standard projection metric appears to over-report single-latent absorption for
a distributed structural feature that has no single-latent causal correlate. As a secondary, controlled result, we show---through
two independent task-accuracy manipulations---that ablation-based absorption is itself sensitive to
task performance, whereas the projection metric is not. We reproduce first-letter absorption and its
dependence on SAE sparsity as pipeline validation. Effects are near-floor and the study spans one
well-powered structural property, one model family, and one SAE recipe; we therefore present the result
as a metric-validity caveat, argue that representational absorption signals require causal validation,
and specify exactly what would be needed to elevate it to a robust claim. An appendix documents three
hypotheses we built controls to test and then rejected.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}
Mechanistic interpretability seeks to reverse-engineer the internal computations of neural networks into
human-understandable parts, both as basic science and as a foundation for auditing and steering models
whose capabilities are hard to trust by behavior alone. A central obstacle is \emph{superposition}: models
appear to represent far more features than they have neurons, packing them into linear combinations of
directions rather than into individual units \citep{elhage2022superposition}. Sparse autoencoders (SAEs)
are the leading proposed remedy: by learning an overcomplete, sparsely activating dictionary that
reconstructs a model's activations, they aim to recover the underlying features as individual, monosemantic
latents \citep{bricken2023monosemanticity,cunningham2023sparse,templeton2024scaling}. Open SAE suites such
as Gemma Scope \citep{lieberum2024gemmascope} have made this decomposition available at scale and turned SAE
features into a common substrate for interpretability tooling.

The value of that substrate rests entirely on the latents meaning what they appear to mean. \emph{Feature
absorption}, identified by \citet{chanin2024absorption}, is a sharp counterexample. A latent may look like a
clean detector for a general concept---say ``the token starts with the letter S''---while in fact it
silently deactivates on particular tokens (e.g.\ \emph{short}), and a more specific, token-aligned latent
absorbs the general direction and carries the concept for those tokens. The ``general'' latent is therefore
not a reliable detector: it has arbitrary, token-dependent blind spots. Absorption is corrosive precisely
because it is invisible to the usual sanity checks---the latent still has high precision and looks
monosemantic---and because it appears to be a systematic consequence of how SAEs trade off sparsity against
reconstruction when features co-occur \citep{chanin2024absorption,chanin2025hedging}.

Because absorption matters for whether we can trust SAE features, how we \emph{measure} it matters too. Yet
two facts about current practice motivate this paper. First, absorption is measured almost entirely on one
task: whether a single-token word starts with a given letter, chosen because it provides clean, objective
ground truth. Benchmarks instantiate absorption on first-letter spelling and nothing else
\citep{karvonen2025saebench}, and \citet{chanin2024absorption} explicitly flag ``examples of feature
absorption unrelated to character identification'' as open. Second, two different metrics are in use. The
original \citep{chanin2024absorption} is \emph{causal}: it uses attribution to ask whether a single latent
actually mediates the model's answer. SAEBench \citep{karvonen2025saebench} standardized a
\emph{representational} alternative---a projection view already present in \citet{chanin2024absorption}---
that decomposes the residual stream's projection onto a concept
direction into per-latent contributions, adopting it because the causal/ablation signal ``goes to zero in
later layers.'' The representational metric is now the field standard; the ablation metric is effectively
deprecated.

These two facts combine into a simple, untested question:
\begin{quote}
\itshape Do the two standard absorption metrics still agree once absorption is measured off the spelling
task, on a structurally different property?
\end{quote}
We study this for objective, non-spelling token properties---primarily \iscap{}, whether a single-token
word begins with an uppercase letter---and find that the metrics \emph{disagree}. The representational
metric reports (weak, above-null) single-latent absorption for \iscap{}; the causal metric reports a clean
zero. We then spend most of the paper ruling out the boring reasons for that zero, because one of them---
``the causal metric is known to die in later layers''---is a documented artifact rather than evidence of
no absorption. Using a cross-model control (Gemma-2-9b, which performs the task much better than 2b) and a
layer- and accuracy-matched spelling control, we show the zero is genuine: it is neither a task-performance
artifact nor a dead-layer artifact.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.4em,itemsep=2pt]
  \item \textbf{A cross-metric comparison off the spelling task.} We run \emph{both} standard absorption
  metrics---the SAEBench projection metric and the Chanin ablation metric---on structural token properties
  and contrast them directly. We are careful not to over-claim novelty: \citet{chanin2025hedging} already
  run $k$-sparse probing on part-of-speech, so this is not the first look at non-character features; it is,
  to our knowledge, the first \emph{direct contrast of the two absorption metrics} on such features
  (\S\ref{sec:related}).
  \item \textbf{A metric disagreement, with confidence intervals.} For \iscap{}, projection reports
  above-null absorption while ablation reports zero on \emph{every} candidate token ($0/727$ on
  Gemma-2-2b, $0/273$ on Gemma-2-9b); we report CIs and candidate counts throughout, because the effect
  is near-floor (\S\ref{sec:proj},\,\ref{sec:abl}).
  \item \textbf{Controls that isolate the cause.} A cross-model task-ability control and a layer- and
  accuracy-matched spelling control together rule out both ``the model cannot do the task'' and ``the layer
  is dead'' (\S\ref{sec:controls}).
  \item \textbf{A reliability caveat for the ablation metric.} Two independent task-accuracy manipulations
  show ablation-based absorption tracks task performance while the projection metric does not
  (\S\ref{sec:mech}).
  \item \textbf{Reproducibility and honesty.} We release all code, a model-agnostic re-implementation used
  for the cross-model controls, an independently review-verified single-class metric port, and
  Appendix~\ref{app:rejected}, which documents three hypotheses we built controls to test and rejected.
\end{enumerate}

We emphasize up front that the result is small: the effects are near-floor, only one structural property is
well-powered, and both models come from one family. We treat it as a metric-validity caveat rather than a
discovery, and \S\ref{sec:concl} states exactly what would make it robust.

\section{Preliminaries and Notation}\label{sec:prelim}
\paragraph{Sparse autoencoders.}
Let $\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}$ be a residual-stream activation of a transformer at a fixed layer and token
position. An SAE with $m\gg d$ latents encodes and decodes
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{a} = \sigma\!\left(W_{\mathrm{enc}}\,\mathbf{x} + \mathbf{b}_{\mathrm{enc}}\right)\in\mathbb{R}^{m}_{\ge 0},
\qquad
\hat{\mathbf{x}} = \Wdec\,\mathbf{a} + \mathbf{b}_{\mathrm{dec}},
\end{equation}
trained to minimize reconstruction error under a sparsity penalty, so that only a small number $L_0 =
\lVert \mathbf{a}\rVert_0$ of latents are active per input. We write $\Wdec[i]\in\mathbb{R}^{d}$ for the
decoder direction of latent $i$ and $a_i$ for its activation. We use Gemma Scope JumpReLU SAEs
\citep{lieberum2024gemmascope} on Gemma-2 \citep{gemmateam2024gemma2}; the SAEs are folded so that decoder
directions are unit norm, with the norm absorbed into $a_i$, so $\hat{\mathbf x}=\sum_i a_i\,\Wdec[i]$ up to
bias and reconstruction error.

\paragraph{Concepts, probes, and main latents.}
A \emph{concept} is a binary or categorical property of the input token with objective ground truth
(e.g.\ ``the first letter is S,'' or ``the token is capitalized''). For each concept $c$ we train a linear
probe on the residual stream and take its (unit) weight vector $\hat{\mathbf p}_c$ as the ground-truth
concept direction, following the linear representation hypothesis that concepts are encoded as directions
in activation space \citep{park2024linear,elhage2022superposition}. Running $k$-sparse probing over SAE latents identifies the concept's \emph{main
latent(s)} $M_c\subseteq\{1,\dots,m\}$: the small set that a sparse probe uses to predict $c$, grown by a
feature-splitting criterion (extend $k$ while validation $F_1$ jumps by at least $0.03$).

\paragraph{Feature absorption.}
\begin{definition}[Feature absorption, informal]
On a token $t$ whose concept $c$ holds and whose residual-stream probe fires ($\hat{\mathbf p}_c\cdot
\mathbf{x}_t>0$), absorption occurs when the main latents $M_c$ are \emph{silent} ($a_i<\eps$ for all
$i\in M_c$) yet the concept is nonetheless present, carried by some other, more specific latent. Such tokens
are \emph{candidate false negatives}: the general latent misses them.
\end{definition}
The two metrics differ in how they decide whether a candidate is genuinely absorbed.

\paragraph{The ablation (behavioral) metric.}
\citet{chanin2024absorption} operationalize absorption \emph{causally}. On a candidate token, they use
integrated-gradient attribution of the SAE latents to a task metric $g(\cdot)$ (a logit difference for the
correct answer, e.g.\ the target-letter logit minus the mean of the other letters). A token is counted as
absorbed if (i) the main latents are silent, (ii) a single latent has the most-negative attribution (its
ablation most hurts the answer) by a clear margin, and (iii) that latent is aligned with the probe
direction. This is a statement about what the model \emph{uses} to produce its answer.

\paragraph{The projection (representational) metric.}
SAEBench \citep{karvonen2025saebench} instead decomposes the concept content of the residual. Writing the
projection of $\mathbf{x}_t$ onto the concept direction as
\begin{equation}
\hat{\mathbf p}_c\cdot \mathbf{x}_t \;=\; \sum_{i=1}^{m} \underbrace{a_i\,\big(\Wdec[i]\cdot\hat{\mathbf p}_c\big)}_{\textstyle c_i:\ \text{latent }i\text{'s contribution}} \;+\;(\text{bias, error}),
\label{eq:proj}
\end{equation}
a candidate is counted as absorbed if, with the main latents silent, one non-main latent \emph{dominates}
the projection. This is a statement about how the concept is \emph{represented}, independent of whether the
model behaviorally uses it. SAEBench adopted this form because the ablation effect ``goes to zero in later
layers,'' i.e.\ the causal metric becomes unreliable with depth---a point we return to repeatedly, because
it means ``ablation reads zero'' is, in later layers, a known artifact.

\paragraph{Two knobs we make explicit.}
Because SAE codes are sparse, at any token the projection~\eqref{eq:proj} is dominated by whichever few
latents are active; ``one dominant latent'' can therefore arise partly from sparsity geometry rather than
from concept-specific absorption. We control for this with a \emph{random-direction null} (replace
$\hat{\mathbf p}_c$ with random unit directions) and with a scale-free \emph{dominance ratio} $R$: a token is
``single-dominant'' iff $c_{(1)}\ge R\,c_{(2)}$ for the top two contributions. We report rates at
$R\in\{1.5,2,3\}$ and condition on the main-latent-silent pool, so the rate does not bake in main-latent
quality (Appendix~\ref{app:rejected} explains why this matters).

\section{Related Work}\label{sec:related}
\paragraph{SAEs and the monosemanticity program.}
The idea that overcomplete sparse dictionaries can disentangle superposed features
\citep{elhage2022superposition} was turned into a practical LM tool by \citet{bricken2023monosemanticity}
and \citet{cunningham2023sparse}, then scaled to frontier models \citep{templeton2024scaling} and released
openly for Gemma-2 as Gemma Scope \citep{lieberum2024gemmascope}. Our work takes these SAEs as given and
studies a property of the \emph{evaluation} of their features.

\paragraph{Feature splitting, absorption, and hedging.}
\citet{chanin2024absorption} define feature splitting (a latent fragments into several as width grows) and
the pathological special case of absorption, giving the ablation metric and the first-letter benchmark.
\citet{chanin2025hedging} describe \emph{feature hedging}: when correlated features co-occur, a narrow SAE
merges them into distributed, partly polysemantic latents. Hedging is directly relevant to us: it is the
most natural mechanism by which a broadly co-occurring concept such as capitalization would be
\emph{represented but distributed}, so that a ``single dominant latent'' is a weak, partly geometric signal
rather than a token-aligned absorber. Notably, \citet{chanin2025hedging} already probe part-of-speech with
$k$-sparse probing, which is why we restrict our novelty claim to the \emph{contrast of the two absorption
metrics} on non-spelling properties, not to touching non-spelling properties at all.

\paragraph{SAE evaluation and its reliability.}
SAEBench \citep{karvonen2025saebench} standardizes a battery of SAE metrics, including the projection-based
absorption score we scrutinize, and documents the layer-decay of the ablation metric that we control for.
A growing line of work questions whether SAE metrics and features are as reliable or as principled as hoped.
Closest to our concern, \citet{chanin2026benchmarks} audits SAEBench's own quality metrics and argues that
several should not be trusted as stated---our result is a small, specific instance of that broader worry,
aimed at the absorption metric. \citet{leask2025canonical} show SAE features are neither complete nor atomic
(the same dictionary re-seeded yields different ``units''), and \citet{engels2024darkmatter} characterize a
systematically missed error component; both undercut the very premise that absorption is defined against:
that SAE latents are the canonical units of analysis. On the utility side, \citet{kantamneni2025saeprobing} find
SAE latents do not beat raw-activation linear probes on sparse-probing tasks, situating the downstream
``so what'' we do not provide inside a live debate about SAE usefulness. Finally, several architectures
target absorption directly: Matryoshka SAEs \citep{bussmann2025matryoshka} impose a nested feature
hierarchy, OrtSAE \citep{korznikov2025ortsae} penalizes decoder cosine similarity, and the recent
C2R \citep{jin2026c2r} adds a cross-sample consistency regularizer; whether such mitigations change the
projection--ablation disagreement is a natural extension we leave to future work (\S\ref{sec:concl}).

\paragraph{Representational vs.\ causal evidence.}
Our headline is an instance of a broader methodological point, increasingly emphasized in the field:
representational evidence (a probe or projection finding a concept in a direction) need not imply a causal
role, and interpretability claims that rest on it can fail to generalize unless grounded causally
\citep{joshi2026causality}. \citet{lin2026disclose} argue, in almost exactly our terms, that many mechanistic
claims report faithfulness/ablation/monosemanticity as \emph{validation} without stating the assumptions
that would make them \emph{identifying}---an absorption metric is exactly such a validation, not a causal
guarantee. This is the standard motivation for activation patching and causal-mediation analysis (e.g.\
\citealp{meng2022rome}), and it is why the original absorption metric was causal in the first place. Our
contribution is the concrete observation that, for a distributed structural feature, the representational
absorption signal survives while the causal one vanishes even where the causal metric is valid.

\section{Methods}\label{sec:methods}
\paragraph{Models, SAEs, and layers.}
We use Gemma-2-2b at residual-stream layer $12$ and Gemma-2-9b at layer $20$, each with the Gemma Scope
JumpReLU residual SAE of width $16$k at an average $L_0$ near $68$--$82$. Both layers sit well below the
depth at which the ablation metric is known to fail. All activations are read at the word-token position of
an in-context prompt.

\paragraph{Properties.}
Concepts are elicited by in-context tasks with objective, tokenizer-derivable labels over the set of
single-token, purely alphabetic vocabulary items ($\approx 1.7\times10^5$ tokens). We study two spelling
concepts---first-letter and last-letter (26-way)---and four binary structural concepts: \iscap{} (begins
with an uppercase letter), \allcaps{} (all uppercase), \suffing{}, and \suffed{}. Only \iscap{} yields a
well-powered candidate pool ($727$ candidate false negatives on Gemma-2-2b, $273$ on Gemma-2-9b);
\allcaps{} and the suffix properties yield $n{=}9$--$33$ and are reported as anecdotes, not evidence. \textsf{is-numeric} was dropped because Gemma's vocabulary contains
almost no pure-digit single tokens---itself a label-availability limit worth recording.

\paragraph{Pipeline.}
For each concept we (1) train a linear probe on the residual stream at the word position; (2) run $k$-sparse
probing to obtain the main latents $M_c$ via the feature-splitting $F_1$ criterion; and (3) collect the
candidate false negatives---tokens where the probe fires and the true label holds but $M_c$'s sparse probe
does not. Absorption is then computed on these candidates by each metric. The candidate pool size varies by
model and metric, and we report the relevant $n$ with every rate: for \iscap{} there are $727$ candidate
false negatives on Gemma-2-2b, of which a sample of $200$ enters the ablation funnel
(Table~\ref{tab:funnel}) and the $130$ that are additionally main-latent-silent enter the conditioned
projection rate (Table~\ref{tab:proj}); on Gemma-2-9b there are $273$ candidates. The projection and
ablation rates for a given model are therefore computed on the \emph{same} candidate set, with the
projection metric further conditioned on the main-silent subset. For binary properties we use a
single-class re-implementation of the 26-way pipeline; it was independently reviewed for faithfulness to the
reference implementation (a line-by-line check of candidate selection, the denominator, and the
absorption predicate).

\paragraph{Ablation metric.}
We follow \citet{chanin2024absorption}: on each candidate, integrated-gradient attribution of SAE latents to
the task logit-difference, counting a token as absorbed if the main latents are silent, the single
most causal latent is clearly dominant (an additive attribution-gap threshold), and it is probe-aligned
($\cos\ge 0.025$). The rate is (absorbed / probe-true-positives). For the binary True/False tasks the metric
is the True-minus-False answer-logit.

\paragraph{Projection metric.}
We compute the per-latent contributions of Eq.~\eqref{eq:proj} at the candidate word position, exclude the
main latents, and count a token as single-dominant-absorbed iff $c_{(1)}>0$ and $c_{(1)}\ge R\,c_{(2)}$. We
report the \emph{conditioned} rate over the main-silent pool and a random-direction null (mean over five
random unit directions), at $R\in\{1.5,2,3\}$.

\paragraph{Task accuracy and controls.}
For each (property, model) we measure the model's in-context task accuracy. To decide whether an ablation
zero means ``not causally absorbed'' versus one of two confounds, we use: (a) a \textbf{cross-model} control
---Gemma-2-9b performs \iscap{} far better than 2b; (b) a \textbf{layer-matched} spelling control---
first-letter ablation absorption at the same 9b layer/$L_0$; and (c) a \textbf{matched-accuracy} control---
we sweep first-letter task accuracy by varying the number of in-context examples and, separately, by
corrupting a deterministic fraction of in-context answers, so spelling and structural absorption can be
compared at equal accuracy.

\paragraph{Statistics and implementation.}
All rates carry $95\%$ confidence intervals---Wilson score intervals for the conditioned projection rates
and exact/binomial intervals for the behavioral rates---and we report candidate counts everywhere because
effects are near-floor. Experiments ran on a single NVIDIA A100
(40\,GB) via a GPU cluster; Gemma-2-9b runs in bf16. Appendix~\ref{app:repro} gives the full configuration.

\section{Results}\label{sec:results}

\subsection{Reproduction and the $L_0$ law}\label{sec:repro}
As pipeline validation we first reproduce first-letter absorption on current Gemma Scope SAEs and recover
its known dependence on SAE sparsity. Here and in Table~\ref{tab:l0} the metric is the \emph{ablation}
full-absorption rate (SAEBench's \code{mean\_full\_absorption\_score}, computed over probe true positives),
so it is directly comparable to the SAEBench reference but is a different quantity, on a different scale,
from the conditioned single-dominant projection rate of \S\ref{sec:proj}. Absorption rises steeply as $L_0$
falls (Figure~\ref{fig:l0}, Table~\ref{tab:l0}), and sits a consistent factor below SAEBench's own JumpReLU
numbers---as expected for the more thoroughly trained Gemma Scope SAEs. Last-letter behaves like first-letter
(Figure~\ref{fig:prop}), so within the spelling family the phenomenon is not specific to the first character.

\subsection{The projection metric on structural properties}\label{sec:proj}
Table~\ref{tab:proj} and Figure~\ref{fig:probedir} report the projection metric with its random-direction
null across dominance thresholds. For spelling, projection absorption is large and far above the null at
every $R$ (e.g.\ $0.53$ vs.\ $0.04$ at $R{=}3$). For \iscap{}, projection absorption is above the null but
\emph{marginal} at $n{=}130$: the strict $R{=}3$ rate is $0.12$ against a null of $0.03$ (a $3.6\times$
ratio), and its Wilson $95\%$ CI $[0.077,0.191]$ excludes the null point estimate but is wide at this
sample size (Table~\ref{tab:proj-ci}); a direct paired-difference test, which we do not run here, would be
needed to establish the separation firmly. It is also roughly $4\times$ weaker than spelling. The remaining
structural properties point the same way but are underpowered ($n{=}9$--$16$) and we do not lean on them.
Read alone, the projection metric therefore says: structural concepts show weak-but-present single-latent
absorption.

\subsection{The ablation metric reports a clean zero}\label{sec:abl}
The ablation metric tells the opposite story. For \iscap{} it reports absorption of exactly $0.0$ on every
candidate token---$0/727$ on Gemma-2-2b and $0/273$ on Gemma-2-9b (binomial $95\%$ CI $[0,0.011]$ for the
9b pool). The probe and main latent are excellent in both cases (probe AUROC $\approx 1.00$, $k$-sparse
$F_1\approx 0.99$), so this is not a failure to find the concept---the concept is cleanly present and cleanly
detectable; it simply is not carried causally by a single absorbing latent.

Table~\ref{tab:funnel} shows \emph{why} the ablation metric returns zero, by tracing where candidates drop
out of the absorption predicate on 2b. Essentially all sampled candidates have the main latent silent
\emph{and} a most-negative-attribution latent (so there is causal structure to test), but almost none have
that latent aligned with the concept direction, and---even among those---the attribution is spread across
many latents rather than concentrated in one. In other words, when the general capitalization latent misses
a token, the model recovers the answer from a diffuse set of small contributions, not from a single
token-aligned absorber. This diffuse recovery is exactly what the projection metric's ``single dominant
latent'' criterion counts as (weak) absorption but the causal criterion does not.

\subsection{Controls: not task-validity, not a dead layer}\label{sec:controls}
The decisive worry is that the ablation zero is the known later-layer artifact rather than a genuine absence
of causal absorption. Two controls rule this out (Table~\ref{tab:cross}, Figure~\ref{fig:cross}).

\emph{Task ability.} Gemma-2-9b performs \iscap{} at $0.91$ in-context accuracy, versus $0.59$ for
Gemma-2-2b (both from the balanced-ICL row of Appendix~\ref{app:extended}, Table~\ref{tab:acc}). If the zero were because the model could not
do the task and so provided no behavioral signal to attribute, it should lift on 9b. It does not: 9b \iscap{}
ablation absorption is again $0.0$, on all $273$ candidates.

\emph{Layer validity.} At the \emph{same} 9b layer and $L_0$, first-letter ablation absorption is $0.045$---
higher than 2b's $0.032$---so the ablation metric demonstrably fires at that operating point. The zero is
therefore specific to the structural property, not the layer.

\emph{Matched accuracy.} Finally, using the accuracy sweep of \S\ref{sec:mech}, at matched task accuracy
${\sim}0.9$ (bracketing the 9b \iscap{} accuracy of $0.91$) first-letter ablation absorption is
${\sim}0.017$--$0.035$ depending on the manipulation (Tables~\ref{tab:iclsweep}--\ref{tab:corrupt}: ICL
gives $0.035$ at accuracy $0.92$, corrupted-ICL gives $0.017$ at accuracy $0.93$), while \iscap{} is $0.0$.
So even holding task ability fixed, spelling absorbs and \iscap{} does not.

\subsection{The ablation metric is itself task-accuracy-sensitive}\label{sec:mech}
The controls above raise a question about the ablation metric in its own right: how much does its reading
depend on the model performing the eliciting task? We answer it by manipulating first-letter task accuracy
two independent ways and watching both metrics (Tables~\ref{tab:iclsweep}--\ref{tab:corrupt},
Figure~\ref{fig:mech}). Reducing the number of in-context examples from $10$ to $0$ drops accuracy from
$0.95$ to $0.53$ and ablation absorption from $0.030$ to $0.011$; corrupting a growing fraction of
in-context answers drops accuracy from $0.97$ to $0.80$ and ablation absorption from $0.030$ to $0.013$. In
both manipulations ablation absorption tracks accuracy, while the projection rate is nearly flat---a small,
same-direction dip from $0.62$ to $0.59$, and to $0.55$ at zero-shot, far smaller than ablation's
${\sim}2\times$ swing. The ablation metric thus largely conflates ``the concept is causally absorbed'' with
``the model performs the task''; the projection metric barely does. This both (i) validates our decision to control task
accuracy in \S\ref{sec:controls} and (ii) is a small, reusable caveat for anyone comparing ablation
absorption across settings of differing difficulty.

\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discuss}
\paragraph{The defensible reading is a metric-validity one.}
For \iscap{}, the SAEBench-standard projection metric's ``single dominant latent'' criterion reads modestly
above chance, yet there is no causal single-latent correlate: the ablation metric---in a regime where it
demonstrably works for spelling and where the model performs the task---reports a clean zero. The narrow,
checkable claim is therefore that the projection metric appears to \emph{over-report} single-latent
absorption for a distributed structural feature whose concept direction is present in the residual but not
concentrated in one causally load-bearing latent. We deliberately avoid the grander phrase ``a
representational--causal dissociation'': the effect is near-floor and rests on one well-powered property.
We are also precise about what the ablation zero does and does not show. The ablation metric is, by
construction, a \emph{single-latent} detector---it counts a token only when one latent both dominates the
attribution and aligns with the probe. Its zero is therefore not evidence that capitalization has no causal
mechanism (the model demonstrably computes the answer); it is evidence that no \emph{single} latent
absorbs the concept---which is exactly the object the projection metric claims to have found.

\paragraph{The most likely mechanism, stated plainly: feature hedging.}
Capitalization is a high-frequency, broadly co-occurring concept. Under feature hedging
\citep{chanin2025hedging}, a $16$k SAE spreads such a concept across several latents, so that ``one dominant
latent by projection geometry'' is a weak, partly geometric artifact rather than a token-aligned absorber
the model uses. We stress that this does not make our result a restatement of hedging: hedging predicts that
capitalization is \emph{represented} in a distributed way, but it does not by itself establish that the
field-standard absorption \emph{metric} scores that distributed representation as single-latent absorption,
nor that the ablation metric denies it---the metric-level disagreement is the specific thing our cross-metric
contrast demonstrates. More broadly, some concepts are represented in genuinely multi-latent or multi-dimensional
ways \citep{engels2024multidim}, in which case a metric premised on a single absorbing latent is simply the
wrong instrument, and its ``above-null'' reading reflects geometry rather than a pathology. Our funnel (Table~\ref{tab:funnel}) is consistent with this: when the main latent misses a
token, the concept is recovered by many small, mostly unaligned contributions. We cannot fully separate
``the projection metric over-reports'' from ``hedging makes the projection metric's premise inapplicable
here''---but for a practitioner the operational consequence is the same: a projection-flagged single-latent
absorption for a distributed feature should not be trusted without causal confirmation.

\paragraph{Implications for SAE evaluation.}
Absorption scores are used to compare SAEs and to motivate architectures such as Matryoshka
\citep{bussmann2025matryoshka}. If the projection metric over-reports for distributed features, then
absorption comparisons that include non-spelling concepts could reward or penalize SAEs for a
partly geometric signal. Our result is a small piece of evidence for a general prudence already visible in
the field \citep{kantamneni2025saeprobing}: representational SAE metrics benefit from causal cross-checks.

\section{Limitations and Threats to Validity}\label{sec:limits}
We state the weaknesses plainly.
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em,itemsep=2pt]
  \item \textbf{Near-floor effects.} Spelling ablation absorption is only ${\sim}0.01$--$0.05$ anywhere, and
  the \iscap{} projection signal is marginal above its null at $n{=}130$. The whole disagreement hinges on
  distinguishing a few percent from zero; we report CIs, but the numbers are small.
  \item \textbf{One well-powered property.} Only \iscap{} is robust; \allcaps{} and the suffix properties
  have $n{=}9$--$33$ and are anecdotal. ``Property-type-dependent'' is effectively an $n{=}1$ claim.
  \item \textbf{One model family, one SAE recipe.} Both models are Gemma-2 (cross-\emph{scale} only), all
  SAEs are Gemma Scope JumpReLU. We run no non-JumpReLU or Matryoshka SAE and no non-Gemma model.
  \item \textbf{A single SAE per site, no seed variation.} We use one Gemma Scope SAE per (model, layer) and
  do not vary the training seed. Because re-seeded SAEs need not recover the same latents
  \citep{leask2025canonical}, the single-latent projection signal we scrutinize could itself shift across
  seeds---which, if anything, further undercuts treating it as a stable property of the concept.
  \item \textbf{No downstream consequence.} We do not exhibit a task in which trusting the projection-flagged
  absorption would demonstrably mislead---the ``so what'' that would turn a caveat into an actionable finding.
  \item \textbf{Metric operationalization.} The dominance threshold and the alignment cutoff are choices; we
  mitigate with an $R$-sweep and a random null, but a determined skeptic could probe their sensitivity further.
\end{itemize}

\section{Conclusion and Future Work}\label{sec:concl}
Measuring SAE feature absorption off the first-letter spelling task exposes a disagreement between its two
standard metrics. For \iscap{}, the projection (representational) metric reports weak-but-above-null
single-latent absorption that the ablation (causal) metric denies, and controls show the denial is neither a
task-performance nor a dead-layer artifact. The most likely mechanism is feature hedging, and the honest
headline is a metric-validity caveat: representational absorption signals for distributed structural
features can lack a single-latent causal correlate.

Three concrete steps would turn this single marginal case into a robust, transferable claim, in rough order
of leverage: (1) establish the projection--ablation disagreement on a \emph{second SAE recipe} (e.g.\ TopK,
standard, or Matryoshka) and ideally a second model family, to rule out a Gemma-Scope/JumpReLU artifact and
to test whether an absorption-mitigating architecture changes the disagreement; (2) power up a \emph{second}
structural property (e.g.\ is-plural or is-past-tense at $n>130$), so the effect is property-type-general
rather than a capitalization quirk; and (3) exhibit one \emph{downstream decision}---a probing, steering, or
unlearning use-case---that the mis-measurement would flip. Until then, we offer the result as a controlled,
honestly scoped caveat rather than a discovery.

\section{Hypotheses tested and rejected}\label{app:rejected}
Because this project's value is as much in its controls as in its final number, we record the hypotheses we
built purpose-made experiments to test and then rejected. Each was discarded on evidence, not taste.

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.4em,itemsep=3pt]
  \item \textbf{``Absorption is spelling-specific.''} An early reading of the raw numbers---structural
  ablation absorption $\approx 0$ vs.\ spelling $\approx 0.05$---suggested a contrarian claim that absorption
  simply does not generalize beyond character identification. \emph{Rejected:} a task-validity check showed
  Gemma-2-2b barely performs the binary tasks (\iscap{} accuracy $0.59$), so the ablation zero could be a
  missing-behavioral-signal artifact; and the projection metric with a random-direction null showed
  structural absorption \emph{is} present above chance. The clean ``spelling-specific'' claim did not survive.
  \item \textbf{``The structural zero is a task-validity artifact.''} The natural next hypothesis was that
  ablation reads zero for structural properties only because the model cannot do the task, so a more capable
  model would show absorption. \emph{Rejected:} Gemma-2-9b performs \iscap{} at $0.91$ accuracy yet still
  shows $0.0$ ablation absorption (\S\ref{sec:controls}); and at matched accuracy spelling still absorbs while
  \iscap{} does not. Task validity is real \emph{within} spelling (\S\ref{sec:mech}) but does not explain the
  structural zero. This falsification is what left the narrower, defensible metric-validity claim.
  \item \textbf{A denominator/tautology confound.} Because structural main latents are very clean, they miss
  fewer tokens, giving smaller candidate pools and mechanically lower rates. \emph{Addressed:} we report the
  rate \emph{conditioned} on the main-silent pool (fraction of missed tokens that absorb), which removes the
  main-latent-quality confound; the projection-vs-ablation gap survives conditioning.
\end{enumerate}
A methodological reflection: at three points a suggestive positive reading was available and was retired by a
control. We consider the controls, not the retired claims, the substantive content.
